Generic Prompt Recommendations

  • Three examples to a good prompt =
    • Context: The background information that explains the situation, audience, target domain, or goal. It tells the AI who or what setting it is operating within.
    • Instruction or Task: The clear, specific command telling the AI exactly what task to perform. It often includes guidelines for length, tone, and style.
    • Format or Example: A sample or demonstration of what the ideal output looks like. Providing an example reduces guesswork and steers the AI toward your exact expectations.
  • “Before you start, ask me any clarifying questions and list anything that's unclear or missing.”
  • “Work through this step by step and show your reasoning before you give me the answer.”
  • Be clear and direct. Claude follows explicit instructions well and guesses poorly on purpose. Say the output you want, the audience, the format, and the length.
  • Show it one good example. A single sample RFI response or calc header teaches format faster than three paragraphs describing it.
  • (Optional) Separate your context from your instructions using simple tags. Claude is trained to read them:
    • <project_info> 4-story steel office building, SDC C, Site Class D, IBC 2021 / ASCE 7-22 </project_info>
    • <task> Draft the lateral system narrative for the schematic design report. </task>*
  • Tell Claude what to do when it isn't sure. “If a section number isn't in the text I pasted, say so instead of guessing.” This is the single highest-value line for engineering work.
  • Don't retype context. Stable things (your role, tone, standards, disclaimers) belong in Instructions for Claude or Project instructions, not in every prompt.
  • Prompt about your prompt. “Rewrite this prompt so it's clearer, and tell me what information you'd still need from me.”

1. Your contracts. Check the owner-architect-engineer agreement and any NDA for language on third-party services, data processing, and confidentiality. Some owners — healthcare, K-12, government, secure facilities — restrict or prohibit it outright.

2. Your firm's policy. If there isn't one, that's your finding to take back to leadership. If there is one, know which tools and which categories of data it actually approves.

3. Your professional liability carrier. Several now publish AI guidance, and some ask about AI use at renewal.

4. Your licensure board. Rules on responsible charge and sealing didn't change because the tools improved. You are the engineer of record for everything you seal.5. Disclosure. Decide with your firm whether and how AI use gets disclosed to clients. Contract language on this is starting to appear.

Habits that lower the stakes today:
De-identify. Strip project name, address, owner, and sheet titles when the question is technical. “A 4-story steel office building, SDC C, Site Class D” gets the same answer as the real project name.
Use incognito (Item 1) for one-off sensitive questions.
Firm rollout belongs on a business plan. Team and Enterprise sit under commercial terms with admin control — that's the answer for firm-wide use, not personal accounts holding firm data.
Don't connect Claude to project SharePoint, client systems, or firm email without written approval.		Helpful framing of Claude for Enigeering: Claude is a drafting and research assistant, not a basis of design and not a signatory. Its output has the same status as a textbook, a vendor design guide, or a junior engineer's first pass — an input you check. Your seal means you exercised professional judgment over the result. Nothing about the tool changes who is in responsible charge.

Questions to consider: If a plan reviewer asked how this number was produced, could you walk them through it without mentioning AI at all? If not, you haven't verified it yet.

Would you be comfortable if this chat transcript showed up as an exhibit? That's the real privacy test, and it's stricter than any toggle.

5. Prompting Techniques & Your Profile PromptHave Claude help you build a reusable Profile Prompt, then park it in a Project (Item 6) so you never paste it again. Try this prompt:

Help me build a Profile Prompt I can use as Project instructions in Claude. Ask me clarifying questions one group at a time, then give me the finished block in plain text I can paste. Start from this draft:

<role_and_scope>
I am a Project Structural Engineer. Tools: Microsoft 365, Revit, RISA, Enercalc, SharePoint/Drive, Grasshopper. Typical deliverables: calculations, RFIs, submittal logs, meeting minutes to action logs, options matrices, QA checklists.
</role_and_scope>

<approach_and_tone>
Answer concisely without dropping critical details. Prefer tables for lists. Mark assumptions as [TBD: what]. Quote source and section IDs verbatim when I paste excerpts. Offer 1–2 alternative approaches when relevant.
</approach_and_tone>

<citations>
Cite building codes and standards (IBC, ASCE 7, ACI, AISC, NDS), peer-reviewed or recognized engineering publications, and files I provide. If a source isn't definitive, label it commentary rather than fact. Never invent a section number — if you don't have the text, say so.
</citations>

<limits>
You are not a licensed engineer and won't stamp or sign anything. No client-confidential information. For high-risk tasks (code compliance, sealed documents), return a checklist or QA plan instead of a final decision. Note when an answer is jurisdiction-specific.
</limits>

<response_examples>
Confident: "ASCE 7-22 Table 12.2-1 classifies this as SDC C for Site Class D."
Cautious: "Literature suggests this is common practice, but I couldn't confirm a definitive code requirement. Want me to research it?"
</response_examples>		Why the tags. Claude is specifically trained to respect XML-style tags as section boundaries. They keep your standing context from getting blended into the day's question. Plain headers work too — tags just work better.

Four moves that do most of the work with Claude:
1. Be clear and direct — state the output, audience, format, and length.
2. Give an example — paste one RFI response or calc header you'd be happy to receive.
3. Ask for reasoning — “think it through step by step before answering.”
4. Set the uncertainty rule — “say you don't know rather than guessing.”

Iterate instead of restarting. “That's close. Keep the structure, tighten it to 200 words, and move the assumptions to a bulleted list at the top.”

Watch for sycophancy. If you want a real review, ask for one: “Argue against this approach. What would a peer reviewer flag first?”Prompt engineering guide

8. Reading Drawings, Specs, Submittals & PhotosThe high value daily use task

Upload: click the + to the left of the chat input box > Add files or photos, drag files into the chat window, or paste a screenshot straight from your clipboard.

Limits that matter: up to 20 files per chat, 500 MB each, PDFs up to 1000 pages. Claude reads both text and visuals — drawings, details, charts — on PDFs of 100 pages or fewer. Past 100 pages it's text only, so split a drawing set down to the sheets you actually care about.

Project knowledge is different: 30 MB per file, unlimited files, text extraction only for most formats. So park specs and office standards in Project knowledge (Item 6), and upload drawings into the chat itself.

Try it on something real:
Here's the structural drawing and the shop drawing for the same connection. List every difference you can find. Flag anything you can't read clearly instead of guessing.

Read this spec section and build me a submittal log: spec section, item, submittal type, responsible party. Quote the paragraph number verbatim for every row.

This is a photo of a field condition. Describe what you see, list what a structural engineer should check, and tell me what additional photos or dimensions you'd need before saying anything definitive.

Genuinely good at: pulling schedules and tables out of PDFs, turning a spec section into a checklist, comparing two documents for differences, transcribing a marked-up sketch or your own handwriting, and reading a screenshot of an error or a program output.

Cannot do, no matter how confident it sounds:Scale anything. It cannot measure a dimension off a drawing. If the dimension isn't printed, it doesn't exist.
See what's cropped. A callout running off the edge gets quietly completed. Tell it to flag anything partially visible.
Read bad scans reliably. Vector PDFs out of Revit read well; a 200-dpi scan of a 1970s drawing is a coin flip.

The prompt that changes the hit rate:
Quote the text verbatim and give me the sheet and detail number for each item. If something is illegible, cropped, or ambiguous, list it under “Could not read” instead of interpreting it.

For a full set: don't dump 40 sheets in. Upload the three that matter, or hand the folder to Cowork (Item 16) and let it work sheet by sheet.

12. Tables, Spreadsheets & Calc WorkbooksTables in chat — just ask, and define the columns.Create a table of wide-flange shapes with a moment of inertia between 1400 and 1600 in⁴. Columns: shape, Ix, Zx, weight per foot. Sort lightest first. Use the AISC v16.0 shapes database and tell me if you can't confirm a value.

A real workbook — requires “Code execution and file creation” (Item 3). Try this prompt:

Act as a structural engineering assistant. Create an Excel workbook titled “Uniformly Distributed Simply Supported Beam” with two sheets.

Sheet 1: Inputs. Collect and label with units: w = uniform load (k/ft), L = span length (ft), x = distance from left support (ft), E = modulus of elasticity (ksi), I = moment of inertia (in⁴). Bold headers, one variable per row (Variable, Description, Units, Input cell), light shading on input cells, readable column widths.

Sheet 2: Calculations. Every formula must be a live Excel formula referencing the Inputs sheet (e.g. =Inputs!B2), not a hard-coded value, so results update when inputs change. Compute: reaction R = wL/2; shear Vx = w(L/2 − x); max moment Mmax = wL²/8; moment Mx = wx(L − x)/2; max deflection Δmax = 5wL⁴/(384EI); deflection Δx = wx(L³ − 2Lx² + x³)/(24EI). Watch your units — E is in ksi and I in in⁴, so convert consistently and show the conversion in the sheet.

Label every result, use borders and bold labels, 3 decimal places. Add a note at the bottom citing Roark's Formulas for Stress and Strain and Hibbeler's Mechanics of Materials. Then list what you built in a table: Quantity, Formula, Excel Formula, Result, Units.		Say “live formulas,” not “calculate.” Left alone, an AI will happily hand you a sheet of frozen numbers. The phrase that fixes it: “every calculation must be an Excel formula referencing the Inputs sheet, not a pasted value.”

Pro tip — don't type equations. Snip the formula or the shear/moment diagram from your reference and paste the image into the chat: “Use the attached image for all formulas. Don't search the web for them. Build the workbook titled Uniformly Distributed Simply Supported Beam.” Claude reads images well, which also makes it good at troubleshooting a screenshot of an error.

Or make it validate first: “Find the equations for shear, moment, and deflection for a simply supported beam under uniform load. Use reputable sources, show me the equations and where they came from, and wait for me to confirm before building the spreadsheet.”

Always ask for the audit: “List every assumption you made and every place a unit conversion could be wrong.” Then spot-check one row against a hand calc before anyone else sees it.

13. How Claude Fails — and How You Catch ItTeach the failure modes by name. “Verify the output” changes nobody's behavior; knowing what the failure looks like does.

The five that matter in structural work:
1. Invented or transposed citations. A section number that looks right and isn't — the most checkable failure and the least checked.
2. Plausible table values. A Zx, an R value, a seismic coefficient recalled rather than looked up.
3. Silent unit errors. ksi vs. ksf, in⁴ vs. ft⁴, kips vs. pounds. The tone stays confident while the magnitude goes sideways.
4. Code edition drift. Claude's training has a cutoff date and it doesn't know your jurisdiction's amendments. Ask which edition it's working from, and tell it which one you're on.
5. Agreeableness. Push back on a correct answer and it may fold. Its agreement is not evidence.

The highest-value fix:
Use the code tool for every calculation and show me the code.

Left alone, Claude does arithmetic by reasoning through it token by token. Told to compute in code, it runs actual Python — deterministic, inspectable, and it shows the work. Requires code execution (Item 3).		Make QA a prompt pattern, not a disclaimer. These four, in this order, catch most of it:

Now solve it again using a different method and tell me where the two disagree.

What would have to be true for this answer to be wrong? List the assumptions that would break it.

Act as the reviewer trying to reject this submittal. What do you flag first?

Go back through your answer and mark every number as [calculated], [from the source I gave you], or [from memory]. — that last category is your verification list.

Constrain the source. For code questions, paste the actual provision and add: Use only the text I pasted. If the answer isn't in it, say so rather than filling the gap. This turns the most dangerous failure into a visible refusal.

Context hygiene. Long chats drift and early details go fuzzy. When a thread has wandered: Summarize everything we've settled, including assumptions, so I can start a clean chat. Paste that into a new chat in the same project.

None of this makes Claude reliable. Independent verification by a qualified person is still the check. Claude's job is to get the draft to that review in better shape — and, now and then, to catch what the human missed.

16. Go Agentic: Cowork & Claude Code[Paid plan]

Cowork is Claude working on a task instead of answering a question: describe the outcome, walk away, come back to finished files. Available on desktop, web, mobile, and the Chrome side panel — choose Cowork in the message box, or just describe a multi-step task.

On Claude for Mac/Windows you can connect a local folder so Claude reads and writes files directly instead of you uploading and downloading. Tasks run on Anthropic's servers, so they keep going after you close the laptop.

Claude Code is the terminal version for scripts, macros, Grasshopper/Python, and repositories. The same account, the same skills.		VBA example — batch your beam spreadsheet:
Create a VBA script for my Excel file to batch process multiple entries through the beam workbook. Inputs live in the "Inputs" worksheet, D2:D6 (w, L, x, E, I). Outputs live in the "Calculations" worksheet, D2:D7 (R, Vx, Mmax, Mx, deltaMax, deltaX). The "Summary" worksheet has rows of inputs and empty output columns. For each data row in Summary, write the 5 inputs into Inputs!D2:D6, recalculate the workbook, then write the 6 outputs from Calculations!D2:D7 back into that row. Handle blank rows, and tell me how to install and run it.

Cowork examples:
Here's a folder of concrete mix design submittals. Build me a comparison table with mix ID, strength and age, SCM percentage, and GWP per cubic yard, and flag which ones meet a 300 kg CO₂e/yd³ target.

A Note for Engineers

Claude is a research, drafting, and QA assistant — not a licensed engineer. It won't stamp anything, and neither should you on its say-so. The two habits that make it safe in practice:

  1. Give it the source. Paste the actual code text, spec section, or output file rather than asking from memory. Then tell it: “Use only what I pasted. If it isn't there, say so.”
  1. Verify what matters. Every section number, every formula, every unit conversion that ends up in a deliverable gets checked against the original. Claude is good enough to be convincing when it's wrong — which is exactly why the check is the job.
